Writing Documentation Machines Can Actually Read

September 2026 · Janeera D A · 3 min read
AI Documentation Semantic Structure

Documentation used to have one audience: a person, reading a page. That's not true anymore. Search retrieval, AI assistants, and increasingly autonomous agents read our content too, and they read it very differently from how a person does.

A long article with implicit context, a fact buried three paragraphs in, a diagram carrying meaning that no alt text captures, all of that works fine for a person who scrolls and infers. It falls apart for a system that has to pull out one exact answer and nothing else.

Everyone tells documentation teams to make their content "AI-ready." Almost nobody says what that actually means to do on a Tuesday morning, on a real knowledge base, with real deadlines.

What actually breaks

When I started looking at how well our content holds up under retrieval instead of reading, the same failure patterns kept showing up: headings that describe a section instead of the question it answers, one paragraph trying to serve three different reader intents at once, and facts that only make sense once you've read the two paragraphs before them.

What I'm changing, in order

  • Audit first. Before restructuring anything, check how current content actually retrieves today, and where answers come back incomplete.
  • Semantic structure. Consistent heading hierarchies and explicit context in each section, so a section can stand on its own instead of depending on what came before it.
  • Deliberate chunking. Choosing on purpose where one answer starts and ends, instead of leaving it to whatever a retrieval system happens to slice out. If a chunk cuts a caveat off from the fact it belongs to, the answer that comes back is technically true and still wrong.
  • A light metadata and relationships layer. Tagging how articles connect to each other, which starts to look like a small knowledge graph even without calling it that.
  • Exposure. Adopting llms.txt, an emerging convention for telling language models what your content is and where to find it, so a knowledge base isn't just readable by machines, it's discoverable by them.

The part I'm still proving out

None of this is worth much as an opinion. I'm running it against a real knowledge base and comparing retrieval accuracy and answer completeness before and after each change, and I'll share the actual numbers once I have them, rather than a hunch dressed up as a finding.

Machine-readability turned out to be a writing decision, not an engineering one. The people who already know how to write a clear paragraph are the ones best placed to do this.

If you're restructuring your own docs for the same reason, I'd like to compare notes on what actually moved the needle for you.
